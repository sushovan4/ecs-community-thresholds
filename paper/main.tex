\begin{abstract}
Threshold indicator taxa analysis, gradient forest and breakpoints on
ordination axes all assemble a community threshold from taxon-level
abundance change, and so cannot see a community whose taxa rearrange who
co-occurs with whom while each abundance profile stays put. We summarize
co-occurrence structure by the Euler characteristic of a flag complex on the
taxon correlation graph, indexed by edge count so that connectance is fixed
by construction and only arrangement varies. The indexing is essential:
under a similarity-indexed filtration, edge density alone separates
simulated communities more strongly than the Euler characteristic does. Two
instruments run on this summary. A windowed split statistic, pre-registered
on simulated gradients before any field data were seen, locates the change.
A second test recodes the same data so that taxa, not windows, are the
sample---each taxon a point given by its correlation profile on either side
of a candidate split---and measures how far the two resulting clouds
separate by an intersection Euler characteristic profile. The second detects
better and locates worse, so the procedure has two stages. We map where each
fails: power collapses below about half occupancy, plot-level permutation
over-rejects two- to threefold under spatial confounding, and the
interaction test
is anti-conservative below roughly a hundred units, which sets a design
floor. Applied under pre-registration with dated public amendments, it
detects in seven of nine public gradients where the windowed statistic
detects in two, and in a coastal ecotone whose incumbent change points were
published independently---though the two channels disagree about where.
Because the Euler characteristic is a sum of local curvatures, a detected
change decomposes exactly into per-taxon shares. Python and R
implementations are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reclamation of metal-contaminated land is judged against targets that presume a
response shape. If plant community structure degrades smoothly with soil metal
load, then any reduction in contamination buys a proportionate improvement and
targets can be set anywhere on the gradient. If instead the community reorganizes
at a threshold, then effort spent on the wrong side of that threshold buys very
little, and effort that crosses it buys a great deal. The distinction is
practical before it is theoretical
\citep{groffman2006thresholds}, and on a Superfund landscape it decides where
remediation dollars go.

Detecting such thresholds from community data is well studied. The dominant
approach, Threshold Indicator Taxa Analysis \citep[TITAN;][]{baker2010titan},
scores each taxon's change in abundance and occurrence across every binary
partition of the gradient, building on indicator species analysis
\citep{dufrene1997indval}, then aggregates the taxon-level change points into a
community-level threshold. TITAN is deservedly standard: it is interpretable,
it names the taxa driving the signal, and it has a careful treatment of
uncertainty \citep{king2010titan2}. Its structure also fixes what it can see.
Because the community threshold is assembled from taxon-level scores, a
reorganization that changes how taxa are arranged relative to one another
without shifting individual abundance--occurrence profiles is not, by
construction, what the statistic is built to detect. The method's own authors
have written at length on where it is misapplied \citep{baker2013limits}.

The same observation covers the rest of the standard toolkit. Breakpoint
regression locates a threshold in a chosen univariate response, classically an
ordination axis \citep{toms2003piecewise}, so it sees what the ordination
preserves---dominant compositional turnover---and inherits the ordination's
indifference to how taxa covary around that turnover. Gradient forest
\citep{ellis2012gradientforest} aggregates split importances of taxon-level
response functions, one taxon at a time, and so is taxon-aggregative in the
same sense TITAN is. None of these methods is wrong; they are instruments
pointed at taxon-level change. The blind spot they share is a community whose
taxa keep their individual gradient profiles while rearranging their joint
structure---who co-occurs with whom, and how tightly. The summary-free
statistical route---nonparametric multivariate change-point detection on
the raw plot sequence, of which e-divisive is the standard instance
\citep{matteson2014ediv,james2015ecp}---covers distributional change in
principle, but energy statistics at survey-scale $n$ in taxon-space
dimension turn out to have essentially no power for changes that live in
the covariance structure, a claim Section~\ref{sec:swap} makes quantitative
rather than asserts. Section~\ref{sec:titan}
makes the gap concrete in both directions: on a simulated gradient whose
correlation structure reorganizes while every marginal abundance distribution
is held fixed, TITAN returns a handful of scattered, noise-level flags whose
change points spread across the gradient---no coherent community threshold---while on the converse simulation, an abundance shift with fixed
correlation structure, its indicator taxa recover the threshold
(Section~\ref{sec:titan} reports both instruments on both gradients).

Joint structure is not unstudied territory in ecology---co-occurrence
network analysis is an established practice, particularly in microbial
ecology, where network properties are routinely compared across conditions
and stress gradients \citep{barberan2012networks,faust2012microbial,
devries2018networks}. That literature is where the present method's substrate
comes from, and also where its cautionary tale lives: the network summaries
in common use---connectance, mean degree, modularity computed on graphs
thresholded at a fixed similarity---are exposed to the density confound
demonstrated in Section~\ref{sec:simfail}, and comparisons across conditions
are typically descriptive, without an estimator or a significance framework
for \emph{where} along a gradient the structure changes. What we add to that
practice is the connectance control, an explicit localization estimator with
pre-registered inference, and the demonstration of when a topological summary
carries signal that the similarity distribution itself does not
(Section~\ref{sec:swap}).

We take a different route to the same question. Rather than aggregating
taxon-level responses, we summarize the \emph{shape} of the community's
co-occurrence structure with a single integer---its Euler characteristic---and ask where along the gradient that shape changes. The Euler characteristic is
cheap, requires no ordination or distributional assumption, and is stable under
perturbation of the underlying data \citep{dlotko2023eulercurves}. It has been
used to summarize time series \citep{sharma2026ecs}, to give regime transition
in a dynamical system a mathematical definition \citep{majhi2026regime}, and, in
the application that motivates the present construction, to locate the
slug/churn transition in multiphase pipe flow and correct a
forty-year-old industry-standard flow-regime map from thirty-seven trials
\citep{koenig2026churn}. Locating a transition along an ordered parameter is the
same problem in each case; only the substrate differs. One boundary is worth
drawing before any construction is given: the instrument answers \emph{where}
along the gradient the community's joint structure changes fastest, and
whether that change is gradient-associated at all. Whether the change is a
discontinuity in the strict sense---the threshold-versus-smooth dichotomy
of the opening paragraph---turns out, in our own calibration, to demand
more data than the survey designs we study can supply
(Section~\ref{sec:power}), and we are explicit throughout about that limit.

Two things make the transfer non-trivial, and both are reported here because
neither is obvious in advance.

First, \textbf{the naive construction measures connectance, not shape}. If the
Euler characteristic is computed on a thresholded co-occurrence \emph{graph},
then $\chi = V - E$ identically, so the resulting curve is a reparametrization
of the number of edges present and carries no topological information at all.
The summary must be taken on a complex of dimension at least two.

Second, and less obviously, \textbf{even the higher-dimensional summary is
dominated by connectance if the filtration is indexed by similarity}. In
simulation (Section~\ref{sec:sim}) a plain edge-density curve separated two
communities better than the Euler characteristic did, and the topological
signal proved to be the density signal in another coordinate. The remedy is to
index the filtration by the number of edges admitted rather than by a similarity
threshold, which holds connectance fixed by construction and leaves only
structure. Comparing networks at matched density is long-standing practice in
network neuroscience for exactly this reason
\citep{achard2006resilient,vanwijk2010density}; to our knowledge it has not been
combined with an Euler characteristic filtration, nor applied to community
ecology.

A third thing, not anticipated when the study was registered, turned out to
matter more than either: \textbf{what is counted decides what can be
detected}. The natural estimator on the Euler characteristic surface
compares groups of sliding-window curves, and a survey yields about ten
windows however many plots it has, so its evidence is thin by construction.
Recoding the same data so that the \emph{taxa} are the sample---each taxon a
point given by its correlation profile on either side of a candidate split,
and the test a measurement of how far the two resulting clouds have
separated---raises the sample size from ten to the number of taxa, and with
it the power to detect a reorganization on dense data at larger designs
(Section~\ref{sec:mixup}). The gain is bounded and we are specific about its
bounds: it is a factor of roughly $1.3$--$1.7$ rather than a doubling, it
vanishes below about half occupancy, and the recoded test is
anti-conservative below roughly a hundred units, which sets a design floor
(Section~\ref{sec:mixloc}). The two instruments are also not
interchangeable: the new one detects better and locates worse, so the
procedure we recommend, and apply, has two stages
(Section~\ref{sec:twostage}).

This paper contributes, in order: (i) the demonstration that similarity-indexed
topological summaries of co-occurrence structure are connectance measurements in
disguise, and the connectance-controlled construction that repairs them
(Sections~\ref{sec:complex}--\ref{sec:connectance}, \ref{sec:simfail});
(ii) a threshold estimator on the resulting Euler characteristic surface---window rule, change statistic, bootstrap interval, and permutation test---selected on simulated gradients with known thresholds and stated precisely
enough to be pre-registered, which it is, here, before any field data was seen
(Section~\ref{sec:est}); (iii) the interaction test built on the intersection
Euler characteristic profile, which takes taxa rather than windows as its
sample, together with the invalid null that the construction invites and the
calibration that shows the valid one holds at the unit counts where it is
applied (Sections~\ref{sec:mixup}, \ref{sec:mixloc}); (iv) a simulation study giving the estimators'
recovery error, their power as a function of survey design, a negative result
on a proposed sharpness diagnostic, and an operating range---effect size,
taxon count, data type, threshold position, and the cost of spatial
confounding---that says where each instrument stops working
(Sections~\ref{sec:simgrad}--\ref{sec:mixloc}); (v) evidence that the method and taxon-aggregative detection
are complementary in both directions (Section~\ref{sec:titan}); (vi) an exact attribution of a detected change to individual taxa through
the discrete Gauss--Bonnet decomposition of $\chi$
(Section~\ref{sec:curvature}), which on real data names taxa disjoint from
those a taxon-level method names; (vii) pre-registered applications to
public gradients and long-term series, reported in full whether they
detect or not, and the pre-registered cross-dataset test of whether
structure changes before composition (Sections~\ref{sec:public},
\ref{sec:program}, \ref{sec:ordering}); and
(viii) the pre-registered analysis protocol for the Butte Hill restoration
network (Section~\ref{sec:butte}).

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data and notation}
\label{sec:data}

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{S \times P}$ record the abundance of $S$ taxa
across $P$ plots, and let $z \in \mathbb{R}^{P}$ give each plot's position on the
environmental gradient---here soil metal concentration. Write $X_{\cdot A}$ for
the submatrix on a plot subset $A$. Everything below is agnostic to the survey's
specifics; the design guidance in Section~\ref{sec:power} says what $P$ buys.

\subsection{From abundance to a filtered complex}
\label{sec:complex}

Each taxon is represented by its abundance profile across the plots in $A$,
giving a point cloud of $S$ points in $\mathbb{R}^{|A|}$. We use correlation
distance
\[
  d(i,j) \;=\; 1 - \mathrm{corr}\!\left(X_{i A},\, X_{j A}\right)
  \;\in\; [0,2],
\]
which is invariant to each taxon's overall abundance and therefore comparable
across plot subsets of differing productivity. A taxon with no variation
within $A$ (typically, absent from every plot in it) has undefined
correlation; it is assigned distance $1$ to every other taxon, so that it
enters the filtration only after all positively correlated pairs.

\begin{remark}[Closure]
\label{rem:closure}
Reading a correlation matrix invites the oldest objection in the subject:
correlations among the parts of a constant-sum composition are negatively
biased by the constraint itself \citep{pearson1897spurious}, which is why
co-occurrence inference on relative abundances is done in log-ratio
coordinates \citep{aitchison1982, gloor2017microbiome} or with estimators
built for the problem \citep{friedman2012sparcc, kurtz2015spieceasi}.
Nothing in the connectance control addresses this: indexing by edge count
fixes how many pairs enter the complex, not which pairs are most
correlated. Section~\ref{sec:closure} takes the objection seriously enough
to simulate it, and states the conditions under which it does and does not
threaten the inference.
\end{remark}

For a threshold $r$ let $G(r)$ be the graph on the $S$ taxa with an edge wherever
$d(i,j) \le r$, and let $K(r)$ be its flag complex: the simplicial complex whose
$k$-simplices are the $(k+1)$-cliques of $G(r)$. The Euler characteristic is the
alternating sum of simplex counts,
\[
  \chi\bigl(K(r)\bigr) \;=\; \sum_{k \ge 0} (-1)^{k}\,
  \bigl|\{(k{+}1)\text{-cliques of } G(r)\}\bigr|
  \;=\; V - E + T - \cdots .
\]

\begin{remark}
Truncating at $k \le 1$---that is, taking the graph itself rather than its
flag complex---gives $\chi = V - E$, which for fixed $S$ is an affine
function of the edge count. Any apparent signal in such a curve is
connectance and nothing else. Throughout we compute $\chi$ of the
$3$-skeleton of the flag complex (cliques of at most four taxa). Toward the
dense end of the edge-count grid larger cliques exist, so the quantity is the
Euler characteristic of the truncated complex rather than of the full flag
complex; the $3$-skeleta still form a filtration, and the summary is used
only comparatively, at identical truncation, across windows.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Indexing by connectance}
\label{sec:connectance}

Rather than evaluating $\chi$ on a fixed grid of thresholds $r$, we evaluate it
at a fixed grid of \emph{edge counts}. For a target count $m$, let $r(m)$ be the
$m$-th smallest pairwise distance,\footnote{The released code indexes
distances from zero, so its grid point $m$ admits $m+1$ edges; the fixed
offset of one edge is immaterial and is retained for exact
reproducibility.} and define
\[
  \chi_A(m) \;=\; \chi\bigl(K(r(m))\bigr).
\]
Because $G(r(m))$ has $m$ edges (up to ties) for every plot subset $A$, any difference
between $\chi_A(m)$ and $\chi_B(m)$ is a difference in how those $m$ edges are
arranged, not in how many there are. This is the single design decision on which
the method depends, and Section~\ref{sec:simfail} shows what happens without it.
Throughout we use twelve evenly spaced edge counts running from $2S/3$ to
approximately half of all $\binom{S}{2}$ pairs; the released code
(Section~\ref{sec:avail}) is definitive for the protocol. The grid density
is immaterial: because $\chi(m)$ is a cumulative sum and hence smooth in
$m$, halving or doubling the grid (6 or 24 points) leaves the simulated
recovery and the Everglades point estimate unchanged
(\texttt{results/14\_grid\_sensitivity.txt}).

\subsection{The surface and the pre-registered threshold estimator}
\label{sec:est}

Sliding a window along the gradient $z$ and stacking the resulting curves gives
\[
  \mathcal{E}(m, z) \;=\; \chi_{A(z)}(m),
  \qquad A(z) = \{\,p : z_p \in \text{window at } z\,\},
\]
the Euler characteristic surface over connectance and gradient position. A
threshold is a location where this surface changes fastest along $z$.

The estimator is fixed as follows, every choice having been made on the
simulated gradients of Section~\ref{sec:simgrad}---where the true threshold is
known---and none on field data.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Windows.} Order the plots by $z$. Windows contain
$W = \max(6, \mathrm{round}(P/4))$ consecutive plots and slide by
$\mathrm{round}(W/3)$, each window placed at the mean $z$ of its plots. The
window curves $\chi_{A_1}(\cdot), \dots, \chi_{A_K}(\cdot)$ evaluated on the
common edge-count grid are the data for everything below.
\item \emph{Change statistic and location.} For each candidate split $s$ with at
least two windows on each side, compute the absolute Cohen's $d$ between the
two groups of curves separately at each edge count, and average over the grid:
\[
  D(s) \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal M|}\sum_{m \in \mathcal M}
  \bigl|\,d\bigl(\{\chi_{A_k}(m)\}_{k \le s},\,
               \{\chi_{A_k}(m)\}_{k > s}\bigr)\bigr| .
\]
The threshold estimate $\hat z$ is the midpoint of the window positions
flanking the maximizing split, and $D^\ast = \max_s D(s)$ is the test
statistic. Averaging over the grid, rather than maximizing, is what
stabilizes the location (Section~\ref{sec:simgrad}); maximizing over $m$
inflates small-sample noise, the failure mode documented on real data in
Section~\ref{sec:public}.
\item \emph{Significance.} Permute the plot positions along $z$, rebuild the
windows, and recompute $D^\ast$; with $B = 999$ permutations the $p$-value is
$(1 + \#\{D^\ast_b \ge D^\ast\})/(1+B)$. The permutation destroys the
association between community structure and gradient position while preserving
the window geometry and the connectance control, so this is a test of
\emph{association}, not of sharpness: a smooth structural trend will also
reject, as it should.
\item \emph{Uncertainty.} Resample plots with replacement within
each window, recompute the curves, and re-estimate $\hat z$; the 2.5--97.5
percentile interval over $999$ resamples is the confidence statement. We
attempted to calibrate the \emph{width} of this interval as a sharpness
diagnostic---the hope being that it concentrates on sharp thresholds and
stays wide under smooth change of the same magnitude---and the calibration
failed at survey scale: the widths are indistinguishable at $P \le 80$ and
only begin to separate at $P = 160$ (Section~\ref{sec:power}). The interval is therefore an uncertainty statement
only, and the method offers no test of thresholdness; $\hat z$ locates the
fastest structural change whether or not that change is discontinuous.
\end{enumerate}

The resolution of $\hat z$ is bounded by the window width: with windows
covering a quarter of the gradient, structure inside $\pm W/2$ of the true
threshold is mixed by construction, and the simulations bear this out---recovery error tracks window width, not the choice of statistic. Surveys
wanting sharper localization need more plots, not a cleverer estimator.

\subsection{A second instrument: the interaction test}
\label{sec:mixup}

The estimator of Section~\ref{sec:est} has a structural weakness that no
amount of data repairs. Its unit of evidence is a \emph{window curve}: a
survey of $P$ plots yields about ten windows however large $P$ is, because
the window rule scales the windows with the survey. The two-sample
comparison therefore runs at an effective sample size near ten, and the
permutation test inherits that. Section~\ref{sec:power} measures the
consequence---power $0.66$ at $P = 160$ for a strong reorganization, and
$0.18$ at $P = 80$---and
Section~\ref{sec:limits} shows it collapsing on the sparse count matrices
that real surveys produce.

The remedy is to change what is being counted. Fix a candidate split of the
gradient-ordered units into a low set $A$ and a high set $B$, and let
$C_A, C_B \in [-1,1]^{S \times S}$ be the taxon correlation matrices on the
two sides. Taxon $i$ is then a \emph{pair} of points: its correlation
profile $C_A(i,\cdot)$ below the split and $C_B(i,\cdot)$ above it. The
question ``did the community's joint structure reorganize at this split?''
becomes ``do the two clouds of $S$ taxon-profiles occupy the same region of
profile space?''---and the sample size is now the number of taxa, tens to a
hundred, not the number of windows.

Concretely, pool the $2S$ profiles, project them to $d_0 = 4$ dimensions by
principal components computed on the pooled cloud---label-blind, so the
projection carries no information about which side a point came from---and
write $a, b \subset \mathbb{R}^{d_0}$ for the resulting clouds. For a radius
$r$ let $U(a;r) = \bigcup_{x \in a} B(x,r)$. The \emph{intersection Euler
characteristic profile} is
\[
  \Delta\chi(r) \;=\; \chi\bigl(U(a;r) \cap U(b;r)\bigr)
  \;=\; \chi\bigl(U(a;r)\bigr) + \chi\bigl(U(b;r)\bigr)
      - \chi\bigl(U(a \cup b;r)\bigr),
\]
each term computed exactly from the Alpha complex nerve of the corresponding
ball union \citep{maria2014gudhi}, so the profile costs three Euler
characteristic curves and no persistence computation. The scalar summary is
the normalized integral
\[
  T \;=\; \frac{1}{r_{\max}} \int_0^{r_{\max}} \bigl|\Delta\chi(r)\bigr|\,dr ,
\]
dimensionless, hence invariant to the scale of the profiles; $r_{\max}$ is
set from the clouds' own diameter on a 60-point grid. Two clouds that
interpenetrate share ball unions at small $r$ and give large $T$; two clouds
that have separated intersect only late and give small $T$. \emph{Small $T$
is evidence of reorganization}---the reverse of the usual convention, and a
consequence of measuring overlap rather than difference.

The test statistic is $T^\ast = \min_s T(s)$ over a grid of candidate splits
at unit quantiles, and significance comes from the same permutation
principle used throughout: permute the units' gradient positions, rebuild the
correlation matrices, the projection, the profiles and the minimization, and
count replicates with $T^\ast_b \le T^\ast$. Because every replicate repeats
the minimization, the multiplicity of the split search is paid for exactly,
and the $p$-value is valid whatever the grid contains.

What that argument does and does not deliver is worth stating precisely,
because it is the paper's only inferential claim about this statistic. Under
the null that the units' gradient positions are exchangeable, the
permutation distribution is exact by the usual rank argument, and the
argument is generic---it uses nothing about $\Delta\chi$ beyond its being a
fixed function of the data. Exchangeability is the substantive assumption,
and Section~\ref{sec:limits} shows it failing under regional structure and
what to do about it. Everything beyond exactness---the null moments of
$\Delta\chi$, analytic power, and the behavior of the statistic when Betti
numbers cancel rather than the overlap vanishing---is open, and we make no
claim on it here; the calibration reported below is simulation, not theory.
A dedicated statistical treatment of inference for intersection profiles---exactness in both directions, null moments, and sample-size calculation---is
in preparation separately; this paper deliberately claims none of it.

\begin{remark}
A cheaper null was tried first and is \emph{invalid} in this encoding; we report it because
the failure is instructive and would recur for anyone building this test.
Swapping each taxon's two profiles independently between the two clouds---a
per-taxon cloud swap, not to be confused with the sign-flip calibration used
for paired comparisons in the two-sample literature---appears to give an
exact paired null. It does not: the observed clouds are
internally coherent---all-$A$ against all-$B$---while a flipped cloud is a
mixture of both regimes, so the mixture's overlap is systematically larger
than the observed one whether or not anything changed. On simulated
gradients the cloud-swap null rejected threshold and no-change gradients at
the same rate ($p = 0.005$ on both). The unit permutation, which rebuilds
the coherence structure under the null, is the correct one.
\end{remark}

The invariant itself is not ours to claim. The intersection Euler
characteristic profile, its Euler calculus and its stability are established
in \citet{kawamura2026intersection}, and it has been used to certify when two
evolving point clouds cease to interact \citep{majhi2026regime}. What this
paper contributes is the ecological encoding---taxa as points, correlation
profiles as coordinates, a gradient split as the two clouds---the
gradient-permutation null appropriate to that encoding, which is not the
label-exchangeability null of the two-sample setting, and the demonstration
that together they convert a weak detector into a usable one.

\subsection{Two stages, and which does which job}
\label{sec:twostage}

Detection and localization are different jobs, and Section~\ref{sec:mixloc}
shows that the two instruments are not equally good at both. The procedure
used for every field analysis below, fixed in advance of the results it
produces (Section~\ref{sec:program}), is therefore:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Detect} with the interaction test of Section~\ref{sec:mixup}:
does the joint structure change with the gradient at all? Where the units
are spatially grouped, the within-region permutation of
Section~\ref{sec:limits} is the test of record.
\item \emph{Locate}, when detection succeeds, with the connectance-indexed
split statistic of Section~\ref{sec:est}, which recovers a known threshold
with roughly half the error of the interaction statistic's own located
split (RMSE $0.117$ against $0.207$ at $P = 160$).
\item \emph{Attribute} the located change to taxa through the curvature
decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:curvature}.
\end{enumerate}

Each stage answers the question it is best at, and the division of labor was
settled on simulated gradients with known thresholds rather than on the
field data.

\subsection{Attribution: which taxa carry a change}
\label{sec:curvature}

The Euler characteristic is a sum of local terms. For a simplicial complex
$K$ and a vertex $v$, write $f_k(v)$ for the number of $k$-simplices
containing $v$ and define the curvature
\[
  \kappa(v) \;=\; \sum_{k \ge 0} (-1)^{k}\, \frac{f_k(v)}{k+1}.
\]
Counting each simplex once through each of its vertices gives the discrete
Gauss--Bonnet identity $\chi(K) = \sum_{v} \kappa(v)$
\citep{knill2012gaussbonnet}, which holds exactly for the truncated
complexes used here. A change in $\chi$ between two groups of windows
therefore decomposes exactly into per-taxon contributions, and a detected
threshold can be attributed: each taxon's grid-averaged change in
$\kappa$ is its share of the change in $\chi$, and its permutation
$p$-value comes from the same permutation that tests the threshold itself.
Neither a modularity score nor a distance-distribution summary decomposes
this way, and the attribution answers, in the method's own terms, the
question a taxon-level method answers by construction: which species are
involved.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:sim}

The results run in the order the instruments were built. We first establish
on simulated data that a similarity-indexed summary measures connectance
rather than shape, and that indexing by edge count repairs it
(Sections~\ref{sec:simfail}--\ref{sec:simgrad}); then map where each
instrument works and fails---power, survey design, sparsity, spatial
confounding, compositional closure, and the design floor below which the
interaction test must not be used
(Sections~\ref{sec:power}--\ref{sec:mixloc}); then ask what the topological
summary buys over its natural competitors, and what it shares with
taxon-level detection (Sections~\ref{sec:swap}--\ref{sec:titan}). Only then
do we turn to field data (Sections~\ref{sec:public}--\ref{sec:ecotone}).
Every dataset of the survey programme was registered before it was analyzed;
the two transcriptomic matrices pre-date the registration programme and the
one anchored run performed after its primary result was seen is labelled as
such where it appears.

\subsection{Indexing by similarity fails}
\label{sec:simfail}

Two groups of communities were simulated---120 taxa in eight modules, fifteen
replicate communities per group---differing in how coherently the modules
respond across conditions. Sweeping a fixed similarity grid, edge density alone
separated the two groups at $\max|\mathrm{Cohen}\ d| = 4.59$, against $2.20$
for $\chi$ of the flag complex. Subtracting the Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi expectation
of $\chi$ at the observed edge count---a density-matched null---left the
separation at $2.21$: under a similarity sweep the topological summary carries
no separation beyond what connectance already supplies.

\subsection{Indexing by connectance isolates structure}

At matched edge counts the comparison inverts. Two point configurations in
$\mathbb{R}^3$ (Euclidean distance; 120 points, fifteen replicates each)---eight modules placed independently, and the same modules arranged on a cycle---have identical connectance at every grid point by construction, and the
connectance-indexed $\chi$ separated them at
$\max|\mathrm{Cohen}\ d| = 4.71$ (Figure~\ref{fig:confound}). Once
connectance is controlled, the summary responds to arrangement. Whether it
also carries information that the distance distribution does not is a
separate question, taken up in Section~\ref{sec:swap}.

\subsection{Simulated gradients, and the choice of estimator}
\label{sec:simgrad}

To fix the estimator we simulated gradients with a known threshold. Sixty taxa
in six modules load on latent factors ($\rho = 0.55$); above the threshold
$z^\ast = 0.55$, paired modules begin sharing a factor with mixing weight
$0.7$, so the community reorganizes from six blocks into three super-blocks.
Critically, every taxon's marginal abundance distribution is identical at every
gradient position---unit variance throughout, by construction---so the
gradient carries \emph{only} co-occurrence reorganization, the signal the
methods of Section~\ref{sec:intro} are not built to see. Plots are placed uniformly at
random on the gradient; designs of $P = 40$, $80$, and $160$ plots use the
window rule of Section~\ref{sec:est}.

Three change statistics were candidates: the discrete derivative of the surface
along $z$; the two-sample split, with $|d|$ either maximized or averaged over
the edge-count grid; and a two-segment continuous fit with the break location
shared across the grid. Table~\ref{tab:est} reports recovery of $z^\ast$ over
100 realizations per design, and the mean statistic on threshold,
smooth-change, and no-change gradients.

\subsection{Power, false positives, and sharpness}
\label{sec:power}

This section turns the underpowered negatives of Section~\ref{sec:public}
into design guidance: the question a survey must answer in advance is what plot
count per window resolves an effect worth caring about. We report the
permutation test's rejection rate on sharp-threshold gradients and its false
positive rate on no-change gradients at three design sizes, and the bootstrap
calibration promised in Section~\ref{sec:est}.

At $\alpha = 0.05$ with 499 permutations of plot positions and 50
realizations per cell, the test rejected sharp-threshold gradients at rates
$0.00$, $0.08$, $0.18$, and $0.66$ for $P = 24$, $40$, $80$, and $160$, with
false-positive rates on no-change gradients of $0.08$, $0.02$, $0.02$, and
$0.06$, and rejected the abundance-shift gradient in $0.00$ of runs at
$P = 80$ and $0.04$ at $P = 160$. Three readings. False-positive rates lie
within Monte Carlo error of the nominal level at every size (the standard
error of a rate near $0.05$ is about $0.03$ at 50 realizations), and the
selectivity is as designed.
The absolute power at single-survey scale is modest---roughly a fifth of
strong reorganizations detected at 80 plots---and is reported as a design
constraint: precisely the switchgrass lesson. And power is
bought with plots per window---$0.66$ at $P = 160$---which motivates the
two extensions below, because a monitoring network does not need more land
to reach that regime; it needs its repeat surveys used correctly
(Figure~\ref{fig:design}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/fig2_design.pdf}

Three further design results follow. First, coverage: the 95\% bootstrap
percentile interval contained the true threshold in 20 of 20 realizations at
$P = 80$ (mean width 0.49 of gradient span)---the interval is conservative,
erring wide rather than narrow, consistent with the resolution limit rather
than with over-confidence.

Second, repeat surveys---the result that decides whether the
Section~\ref{sec:butte} protocol is a fair test. The Butte Hill network has
roughly 80 plots and has been monitored since 2015, so repeat surveys may
span up to a decade (the data inventory will confirm how many); we simulated communities
in which a fraction $\tau = 0.3$ of latent community variance is
year-specific and asked what $T$ repeat surveys buy. The answer depends
entirely on how they are used. Over 50 realizations per cell,
\emph{averaging} years within plots gives power $0.28/0.44/0.18/0.10$ at
$T = 1/3/5/10$, with no trend: averaging cleans each plot's measurement,
while the binding noise is the sampling variability of correlations across
the $W$ plots in a window, which it cannot touch. \emph{Stacking}
plot-years as additional samples attacks that term directly---windows carry
$W \times T$ columns and the permutation moves whole plots, so a plot's
years stay together---and gives $0.26/0.32/0.44/0.36$, overtaking averaging
from $T = 5$ ($0.44$ against $0.18$, and $0.36$ against $0.10$ at
$T = 10$), with false-positive rates at or below $0.10$ throughout. The
gain is real but modest, and both schemes stay well below the power that
additional \emph{plots} would buy (Figure~\ref{fig:design}a): repeat
surveys are a weaker substitute for spatial replication, not an equivalent
one. The protocol stacks years rather than averaging them.

The sharpness calibration returned a negative at survey scale, with a first
sign of life beyond it. At $P = 80$ (50 realizations per cell, 200
resamples) the bootstrap interval width on sharp thresholds (mean $0.515$)
is indistinguishable from its width under smooth change of the same
magnitude (mean $0.516$): the width is dominated by the resolution limit of
Section~\ref{sec:est}, not by the shape of the underlying change. At
$P = 160$ a gap begins to open---mean $0.440$ against $0.484$, and, more
tellingly, sharp thresholds produce intervals as tight as $0.145$ while
smooth change never fell below $0.289$---so the diagnostic may become
usable at design sizes beyond typical surveys, but at the sizes this paper
addresses we report the interval as uncertainty only. A survey
hoping to \emph{classify} its gradient response as threshold-like or smooth
needs either substantially more plots than the designs studied here or a
different instrument; what the present method delivers at these sizes is the
location of fastest change and the evidence that structure is
gradient-associated at all.

\subsection{Where the method stops working}
\label{sec:limits}

The design guidance above varies one quantity, the number of plots. A
survey planner needs the rest of the operating range, so we varied each of
the remaining design quantities around the $P = 160$ merge gradient (30
realizations per cell, 99 permutations; Monte Carlo standard error at most
$0.09$).

Figure~\ref{fig:limits} collects the result.

\emph{Effect size.} Power rises with the strength of the reorganization:
$0.17$, $0.23$, $0.57$, $0.80$ as the mixing weight $a$ goes $0.3$, $0.5$,
$0.7$, $0.9$. Weak reorganizations are out of reach at survey scale.

\emph{Number of taxa.} Power is $0.13$ at $S = 12$, $0.47$ at $24$, $0.67$
at $60$ and $0.53$ at $120$; species-poor assemblages carry too few pairs
to shape a complex, and beyond roughly sixty taxa nothing further is
gained. Cost, by contrast, grows steeply with $S$: the clique enumeration
becomes impractical above roughly 150 taxa at these unit counts, which is
why the applications below cap the taxon list.

\emph{Data type---the binding constraint.} All simulations to this point
used Gaussian abundances. Real survey data are sparse counts, so we drew
Poisson-lognormal counts from the same latent structure, at mean occupancy
$0.8$, $0.4$ and $0.15$, and also reduced them to presence/absence. Power
falls from $0.60$ (Gaussian) to $0.37$, $0.13$ and $0.07$, and to $0.13$ on
presence/absence. Zero-heavy counting destroys the correlation structure
the summary reads: at $40\%$ occupancy the method has less than a quarter
of its nominal power. This single fact does more to explain the field
results of Section~\ref{sec:public} than any other quantity we varied, and
it says that the instrument belongs on cover, biomass or dense count data
rather than on sparse incidence records.

\emph{Threshold position.} A threshold in the middle of the gradient is
found ($0.63$ and $0.60$ at $z^\ast = 0.35$ and $0.55$); one near an end is
not ($0.27$ at $0.2$, $0.20$ at $0.8$), because the windows on the short
side are too few to form a split.

\emph{Exchangeability.} Finally we simulated the confound the permutation
assumes away: eight regions whose community structure differs, with region
gradient means assigned at random, so that no within-region association
exists. Plot-level permutation rejected in $0.15$ of 60 null realizations,
three times the nominal rate; permuting within regions restored it to
$0.07$. Where a gradient is spatially structured, the within-region test is
not optional, and every field analysis below reports it.

\subsection{Closure: when compositional bias can and cannot fake a detection}
\label{sec:closure}

Remark~\ref{rem:closure} raises the compositional objection; this section
answers it, and the answer has two halves, one reassuring and one not.

The reassuring half is structural. Closure biases the correlations, but the
permutation null is built from the same biased correlations. Permuting the
units' gradient positions leaves every unit's composition exactly as it was;
only the assignment of units to sides of a split changes. So a closure bias
that is the \emph{same} everywhere along the gradient enters the observed
statistic and each of its replicates identically, and cannot on its own
produce a rejection. This is a real protection, and it is not available to
descriptive network comparisons, which is where most of the compositional
horror stories in the co-occurrence literature come from.

The half we expected not to be reassuring is that the bias need not be
homogeneous.
Its strength depends on the total that the parts are divided by, so if total
abundance, or richness, or sampling completeness changes systematically
along the gradient, then so does the induced correlation structure---and the
two sides of a split then differ for a reason that has nothing to do with
who co-occurs with whom. We expected that to be a genuine failure mode, and
it is the one we set out to measure.

It did not appear. On $P = 160$ units of $S = 60$ \emph{independent} taxa---no co-occurrence structure anywhere, so every rejection is a false
positive---we compared open counts against the same counts divided by their
unit total, with and without a gradient in the total and in richness, and
against centred-log-ratio coordinates
(Table~\ref{tab:closure}; 40 realizations, 199 permutations).

Every closed cell sits within Monte Carlo error of the nominal rate,
including the two built to break it: an eightfold rise in total abundance
along the gradient gives $0.03$, and a rising richness ceiling gives $0.03$.
The permutation null absorbs closure more completely than we expected it
to, and the mechanism is the one given above---the composition of each unit
is carried intact through every replicate, so closure is a property of the
data that the null sees too.

One cell does not behave, and it is the one that was supposed to be the
remedy. Centred-log-ratio coordinates rejected in $0.10$ of null runs, twice
nominal and the worst cell in the table. We do not have a theory for it; the
natural suspect is the zero replacement, which on sparse data invents
structure in the imputed entries that the permutation cannot see as
imputation. The practical reading is that CLR is not a free safety
measure here, and a disagreement between raw and log-ratio analyses should
not be resolved by assuming the log-ratio version is the trustworthy one.

Two consequences for practice follow. First, the substrates used here are mostly
\emph{not} closed: Everglades abundances are counts per sampling throw,
Cedar Creek is biomass in g\,m$^{-2}$, the EPA benthic matrices are counts
per standardized sample, and Niwot is hit counts. These are effort-standardized
densities, free to vary in total, and the constant-sum constraint
does not apply to them. The one genuinely closed substrate in this study is
percent cover, and it is the substrate of two datasets here: the coastal
marsh ecotone of Section~\ref{sec:ecotone} and the CRMS matrix, both of
which detect. Neither detection can be dismissed as a closure artifact on
the evidence of Table~\ref{tab:closure}, but neither is insulated by it
either, since that table simulates closure without a real reorganization
rather than the converse.
Second, where cover or relative abundance \emph{is} the substrate, we report
the log-ratio analysis beside the primary one rather than choosing between
them, and a survey whose total abundance trends strongly along its gradient
should treat a detection on closed data as unresolved until the log-ratio
version agrees.

\subsection{The interaction test: power, calibration, and localization}
\label{sec:mixloc}

The operating range just mapped is the operating range of the window-curve
statistic. We now put the interaction test of Section~\ref{sec:mixup}
through the identical simulations, changing nothing but the statistic:
same gradients, same permutation principle, same $\alpha$.

\emph{Power, and the design floor.} On the merge gradient with 40
realizations and 199 unit permutations, the interaction test rejects at
$0.05$, $0.03$, $0.42$ and $0.72$ for $P = 24$, $40$, $80$ and $160$,
against $0.03$, $0.03$, $0.25$ and $0.57$ for the registered statistic run
at the identical Monte Carlo budget. Those last figures sit below the
$0.00/0.08/0.18/0.66$ of Section~\ref{sec:power} because that section used
499 permutations and 50 realizations where this comparison uses 199 and 40.
The two instruments are compared under matched settings, which is what makes
the ratio meaningful; neither series should be read as the registered
statistic's definitive power. The
gain at the two larger designs is real but modest---roughly a factor
$1.3$--$1.7$, not a doubling---and it comes with a cost that has to be
stated first, because it bounds where the test may be used at all: its
false-positive rates are $0.17$, $0.03$, $0.10$ and $0.03$. At $P = 24$ the
test rejects true nulls more than three times as often as it should, and at
$P = 80$ twice as often. \emph{The interaction test is not calibrated at
small unit counts and must not be used there}; the design floor we can
defend from these simulations is on the order of a hundred units, and the
next paragraph establishes that it is well behaved far above it.

\emph{Calibration at survey scale.} Because the field datasets of
Section~\ref{sec:program} run to hundreds and thousands of units---far
beyond any design size used to develop the test---we checked the null
behavior there directly, on gradients with no threshold and no association
with $z$. Over 40 realizations at 199 permutations the false-positive rate
is $0.05$, $0.05$ and $0.07$ on Gaussian abundances at $P = 400$, $800$ and
$1600$, and $0.03$, $0.07$ and $0.00$ on $40\%$-occupancy counts, with
median null $p$-values between $0.39$ and $0.50$. Seven of the nine spatial
datasets have between $148$ and $3180$ units and sit in that calibrated
regime. The exceptions are stated where they arise rather than here: Niwot
at 81 units, the two Everglades demonstration matrices at 125--126, and all
thirteen temporal series at 20--147, every one of which is read against the
design floor rather than against its $p$-value alone.

\emph{Sparse counts: no advantage.} Section~\ref{sec:limits} found sparse
counting to be what stops the curve statistic. The interaction test does not
repair it. At $P = 160$ its power is $0.72$ on Gaussian abundances
(registered statistic $0.60$) and $0.68$ at $80\%$ occupancy ($0.37$), but
$0.10$ at $40\%$ ($0.13$), $0.03$ at $15\%$ ($0.07$) and $0.10$ on
presence/absence ($0.13$). Below roughly half occupancy the two instruments
are indistinguishable, and if anything the newer one is slightly worse. The
gain is confined to dense data. We report this plainly because an earlier
version of these simulations, computed with inexact geometric predicates,
appeared to show a large advantage on sparse counts; it did not survive the
correction described in Section~\ref{sec:avail}.

\emph{Exchangeability.} Under the eight-region confounding simulation that
made plot-level permutation over-reject threefold, the interaction test's
plot-level false-positive rate is $0.12$ (registered statistic $0.15$) and
its within-region rate $0.03$ ($0.07$). Plot-level permutation is
anti-conservative for both instruments, and the within-region test remains
the test of record wherever the gradient is spatially structured.

\emph{Localization, where it loses.} Detection is not location. On merge
gradients with a known threshold at $z^\ast = 0.55$ (60 realizations), the
split that minimizes $T$ recovers it with RMSE $0.227$ at $P = 80$ and
$0.207$ at $P = 160$; the registered window statistic, on the same
realizations, gives $0.164$ and $0.117$. Both are nearly unbiased
($+0.02$ to $+0.05$), so the difference is variance: minimizing a single
scalar over a split grid is a noisier locator than averaging Cohen's $d$
over a connectance grid. This is the entire reason the procedure of
Section~\ref{sec:twostage} has two stages rather than one, and the division
was fixed from this table before any field dataset was re-analyzed.
Figure~\ref{fig:interaction} collects the four comparisons.

\subsection{The summary is modular---and when topology is necessary}
\label{sec:swap}

Nothing in the estimator of Section~\ref{sec:est} is specific to the Euler
characteristic: it consumes any curve-valued summary evaluated on the
connectance grid. We therefore ran six summaries through the same windows,
split, and permutation scheme---$\chi$ of the flag complex; the Betti curves
$\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$; greedy modularity of the $m$-edge graph, the summary
community ecology would reach for first; and two pure
correlation-distribution summaries, the distance quantile $r(m)$ and the mean
pairwise distance, which together are the concrete form of the objection
``why not just report the correlation distribution?''

The comparison needs gradient types that pull these apart. In the merge
scenario of Section~\ref{sec:simgrad} the correlation distribution and the
arrangement change together, so everything detects and nothing is learned.
We add two scenarios on a fixed module graph of six modules each carrying
two equal cross-module links: a \emph{rewiring} gradient, where the module
graph switches from two triangles to one hexagon at $z^\ast$, holding the
degree structure and hence the expected pairwise-correlation distribution
identical on both sides; and a \emph{strength} gradient, where the module
graph never changes but the cross-link weight halves, which moves the
correlation distribution while preserving the edge ranking and therefore,
at matched connectance, the complex itself. Both use stronger coupling than
Section~\ref{sec:simgrad} ($\rho = 0.75$, mixing $0.9$). One artifact is
built in and reported: windows straddling $z^\ast$ mix the two regimes, so a
diluted signal leaks into distribution summaries even on the rewiring
gradient. Integer-valued Betti curves can have zero within-group variance,
so each summary is standardized and the pooled within-group standard
deviation floored at $0.05$ of the between-window standard deviation; the
floor does not bind for $\chi$.

Table~\ref{tab:swap} reports the comparison at $P = 160$ (100 realizations
for statistics, 50 at 499 permutations for rejection); at $P = 80$ no
summary rejects any structured scenario above $0.12$. The designed asymmetry
is confirmed in both directions. On \emph{strength} the distance quantile
rejects at $0.34$ while $\chi$ sits near its false-alarm rate ($0.12$
against $0.06$): matched-connectance summaries are blind to
distribution-only change. On \emph{rewiring} the roles invert, but
weakly---the shape summaries separate from null ($\chi$ $1.74$ against
$1.38$; modularity $1.97$ against $1.46$) while the distance quantile stays
below its null level, yet nothing clears $\alpha = 0.05$ reliably.
Arrangement-only reorganization is the hardest signal in this study: real in
the statistics, beyond dependable detection at 160 plots. The declared leak
is visible, mean pairwise distance rejecting rewiring at $0.14$ purely
through straddling windows. And where detection occurs, matched-connectance
modularity is at least as strong as $\chi$ (merge statistic $4.28$ against
$3.13$; RMSE $0.074$ against $0.102$)---reported rather than buried, because
the framework is summary-agnostic by design. $\chi$ is retained for its
stability guarantee, its deterministic clique counting and its
transition-localization lineage, not for empirical dominance over its
nearest neighbour.

Persistence deserves its own line, since the Euler curve is the alternating
sum of the Betti curves and a reader may reasonably ask why the richer
invariant is not used. We ran the first persistence landscape of $H_0$ and
of $H_1$, and a total-persistence curve, through the identical estimator on
the identical edge-count filtration ($P = 160$; 100 realizations for the
statistics, 30 with 99 permutations for rejection). On the merge gradient
$\chi$ rejected in $0.53$ of runs against $0.13$ for the $H_1$ landscape
and $0.00$ for total persistence, and localized better (RMSE $0.10$ against
$0.16$ and $0.20$). The $H_0$ landscape is identically zero on this grid,
which is a property of the grid rather than a result: all components have
merged before the first grid point at $2S/3$ edges. At survey sample sizes
the integer-valued Betti and landscape curves are noisy, and the
cancellation in the alternating sum is doing useful work; the poorer
invariant wins.

The summary-free statistical baseline completes the comparison. E-divisive
\citep{matteson2014ediv}, run on the raw ordered plot sequences themselves
(no windows, no summary; \texttt{ecp} with 199 permutations at
$\alpha = 0.05$), detected the merge gradient in 1 of 10 realizations at
$P = 80$---indistinguishable from its 1-in-10 false-alarm rate on the
matched null---and the rewiring gradient in 0 of 10 at both $P = 80$ and
$P = 160$---though Table~\ref{tab:swap} shows that even the shape
summaries, whose statistics do separate there, fall short of dependable
rejection on that hardest signal. This is
the expected behavior of energy statistics at survey-scale sample sizes in
taxon-space dimension: they are dominated by location alternatives, and a
change confined to the covariance structure starves them. The ecology
incumbents fare no better: PERMANOVA and PERMDISP
\citep{anderson2001permanova}, given the \emph{oracle} split at $z^\ast$---their best case, since neither localizes---rejected in 0--2 of 10 runs on
every scenario, signals and nulls alike (Euclidean distances, 199
permutations). Both test location and dispersion of sites in dissimilarity
space, and a reorganization that preserves every plot's marginal
distribution moves neither. The windowed, connectance-controlled summary is
not a convenience layer over standard multivariate testing; at these sizes
it is the difference between an instrument and none.

\subsection{Complementarity with taxon-aggregative detection}
\label{sec:titan}

The claim that the two instrument families see disjoint signals was tested in
both directions on the simulated gradients, five realizations each at $P=80$.
On \emph{reorganization} gradients (correlation structure changes at
$z^\ast$, all marginals fixed), TITAN---run with 250 permutations, 250
bootstrap replicates, purity and reliability cutoffs 0.95---flagged a median
of 4 of 60 taxa per run (range 1--9), with per-run median change points
scattered from 0.26 to 0.85 and \textit{sum(z)} intervals spanning most of the
gradient: background false flags at roughly the rate the cutoffs admit, and no
coherent community threshold. On \emph{abundance-shift} gradients (one
module's ten taxa shift mean abundance at $z^\ast$, correlation structure
fixed), it flagged 11--13 taxa per run, always including taxa of the shifted
module, with median indicator change points of 0.49--0.55 against a true
threshold of 0.55 in every run; the community-level sum($z+$) change point
fell at 0.53--0.56 in three of five runs and at 0.19 and 0.40 in the other
two.

Table~\ref{tab:complement} sets the two side by side. Neither result is a
deficiency of either method; each is an instrument pointed at a different
component of community change. The practical recommendation
falls out directly: run both, and read disagreement as information about
\emph{which kind} of reorganization the gradient is driving. There is also
an ecological reason to want the covariance channel read at all: the
early-warning literature on critical transitions holds that changes in
correlation structure can \emph{precede} the abundance collapses that
taxon-level methods detect \citep{scheffer2009ews}---in which case a
structural threshold would be a leading indicator, a boundary to act at
before species are lost rather than after. That motivation is worth stating
because it is the practical reason to read the covariance channel at all,
and because we registered it as a confirmatory test and it failed:
Section~\ref{sec:ordering} finds no systematic ordering across seven
systems. The Everglades panel, where our estimate does fall on the early
side of TITAN's change point, turns out to be the strongest case in the set
rather than a representative one. The reason to read the covariance channel
is therefore not that it fires first; it is that it fires elsewhere, and on
different species.

\subsection{Two Everglades datasets: the incumbent's benchmark, and a powered panel}
\label{sec:public}

\paragraph{A real ecological gradient: the Everglades phosphorus data.}
The first demonstration is on the incumbent's own ground: the \texttt{glades} dataset shipped with TITAN2---126 marsh sites
by 164 macroinvertebrate taxa along a measured surface-water
total-phosphorus gradient, the demonstration data of \citet{baker2010titan}.
At $P = 126$ the window rule gives 32-site windows. The estimator is applied
as fixed on simulation before this dataset was first analyzed; preprocessing---taxa in at
least five sites, $\log(1+\text{abundance})$, gradient ordered by TP---was
fixed before outcomes were seen. One departure from Section~\ref{sec:est}:
for computational cost the test used 199 rather than 999 permutations and
the interval 199 bootstrap resamples; the Monte Carlo standard error of a
$p$-value near $0.3$ at 199 permutations is about $0.03$, immaterial to the
verdict.

The estimator places $\hat z = 12.5$~$\upmu$g/L TP, with a 95\% bootstrap
interval of $[12.0, 45.3]$. That point lies just below the 90\% bootstrap
interval of TITAN's decliner change point regenerated on the identical matrix
($15.1$, interval $13.2$--$19.9$; increasers $32.5$, interval $21.7$--$34.1$;
87 of 164 taxa pure and reliable) and just above the 10~$\upmu$g/L numeric
criterion that governs the system; our interval contains both of TITAN's
community change points.
But the permutation test does not reject ($p = 0.30$ over 199 permutations;
the distribution baseline $r(m)$ behaves similarly, $\hat z = 16.9$,
$p = 0.37$), and per protocol we therefore report \emph{no detection}. This
is the outcome the power analysis predicts rather than a surprise: 126
single-survey sites sit between the $P = 80$ and $P = 160$ rows of
Section~\ref{sec:power} (rejection $0.18$ and $0.66$ for a strong simulated
effect), so the run bounds the co-occurrence signal in these data rather
than refuting one. An exploratory run of the anchored secondary test of
Section~\ref{sec:butte} on these data---performed after the primary
result was seen, and labeled accordingly---also does not reject ($D = 0.99$
at the boundary nearest TITAN's decliner point, $p = 0.38$): the
non-detection is not merely the multiplicity cost of the free split. What
the run does establish, on the incumbent's own benchmark:
the frozen pipeline executes end to end at real survey scale, its point
estimate lands where the taxon-level evidence and the regulatory criterion
place the threshold region, and the verdict of a pre-registered test is
reported as such---the discipline Section~\ref{sec:butte} commits to for
Butte Hill, where stacked repeat surveys supply the power this
single-survey design lacks. Figure~\ref{fig:glades} shows the run
whole: the standardized window curves climb through the regulatory range,
crossing their mean almost exactly at $\hat z$.

\paragraph{The same benchmark, through the second instrument.} The
interaction test was not part of the amendment that registered the
second pass, because that amendment listed the survey datasets and this one
is the paper's demonstration benchmark. We therefore registered it
separately, in a dated public amendment written before the run, which
recorded the expectation that 126 sites of sparse counts made detection
roughly a coin flip and a null uninformative.

It rejects, at $p = 0.005$ on both split grids, placing its split at
$36.2$~$\upmu$g/L TP---close to TITAN's own increaser change point of
$32.5$ (interval $21.7$--$34.1$) on the identical matrix. The same data that
the windowed statistic could not resolve at $p = 0.30$ yields a detection
once the taxa rather than the windows are the sample, and it does so at a
unit count just above the design floor of Section~\ref{sec:mixloc}. This is
the cleanest available demonstration that the gain is instrumental and not a
matter of which dataset was chosen: same sites, same gradient, same
log-transform, same permutation principle, different estimator. One
difference is not cosmetic and we state it: the interaction test applies the
hundred-taxon cap that every dataset in Section~\ref{sec:program} carries,
so it sees 100 of the 164 taxa the windowed run used. The cap retains the
most widely occurring taxa, and Section~\ref{sec:limits} finds power flat
between 60 and 120 taxa, so we do not think it explains the difference---but
it is a difference, not an identity.

\paragraph{A powered natural gradient: the Everglades long-term panel.}
The Everglades benchmark above is a single survey of 126 sites. The same
wetland is also monitored by two long-term programmes whose data are public
\citep{pintar2026everglades}: in the spatially expansive programme, 148
primary sampling units were surveyed once a year for up to twenty years
(2,470 unit-years, 22 insect taxa), with hydroperiod---days since the site
was last dry---recorded per unit. Stacking the repeat surveys, as
Section~\ref{sec:power} prescribes, puts this design in the regime where
the test has power. The analysis was registered before the data were
downloaded, and the estimator applied unchanged.

The structural change point is $\hat z = 1108$ days since last dry
($95\%$ interval $[175, 1108]$), with $p = 0.045$ over the registered 199
permutations and $p = 0.025$ over 1999 (a precision check of the same
test). Because the units fall in ten regions, the within-region permutation
of Section~\ref{sec:limits} is the test that matters, and it also rejects
($p = 0.040$): the association is not an artifact of regional structure.
The pre-registered secondary test, anchored at TITAN's decliner change
point, rejects as well ($p = 0.005$), though its nearest admissible window
boundary coincides with the free split, so it is not an independent look.
An independent 24-site programme in the same wetland does not reject
($p = 0.61$, as its 6-unit windows predict) but places its point estimate
at 1103 days, within five days of the first.

TITAN on the same matrix places its community change point at 2561 days,
above the structural estimate. Its individual increaser taxa have change
points from 263 to 2561 days, so the two channels overlap in range while
differing in where they concentrate; we do not read a clean ordering from a
single dataset, and Section~\ref{sec:ordering} tests the question across
systems instead.

\paragraph{Which taxa carry the change.} Because $\chi$ is a sum of local
curvatures (Section~\ref{sec:curvature}), the detected change decomposes
exactly into per-taxon contributions, which sum to the change in $\chi$
($+41.8$). Five taxa carry $54\%$ of it: damselflies, \emph{Erythemis},
\emph{Libellula}, \emph{Pelocoris} and \emph{Coryphaeschna ingens}
(individual permutation $p$ between $0.015$ and $0.045$; none survives
Benjamini--Hochberg correction across 22 taxa, minimum $q = 0.18$, so these
are leading contributors rather than individually significant). None is one
of TITAN's eight pure and reliable indicator taxa; the largest share carried
by any indicator is $8\%$, and five of the eight carry $2\%$ or less. The two
instruments name disjoint sets of species on the same data: the taxa whose
abundance tracks the gradient are not the taxa whose co-occurrence
partnerships rearrange.

\paragraph{Two transcriptomic stress tests, reported for completeness.}
Before the survey datasets were assembled, the construction was tried on two
public transcriptomic matrices, on the reasoning that it is agnostic to what
the ``taxa'' are. Neither supports the method and both are reported here
because the programme's rule is that nothing goes in a file drawer.
\emph{Arabidopsis thaliana} expression across 690 accessions
\citep{kawakatsu2016} split by flowering time gave
$\max|\mathrm{Cohen}\ d| = 1.61$ at $p = 0.040$ against a heavy-tailed null
reaching $2.63$---a hint at best, from a single assay condition with no
environmental axis, so the surface degenerates to one curve and the dataset
structurally cannot test the claim. Switchgrass under drought
\citep{meyer2014switchgrass} supplies an ordered diel axis and shows a
localized separation peak of the predicted shape ($0.51$, $2.26$, $0.30$,
$0.27$), but neither the peak ($p = 0.135$) nor its variation along the axis
($p = 0.115$) survives permutation, at six to thirteen samples per cell.
Both are underpowered rather than negative, and the max-statistic whose null
spans $0.59$--$4.26$ is precisely the small-sample instability that motivated
the pooled statistic. Full outputs are in the repository.

\subsection{The two-stage instrument on nine public gradients}
\label{sec:program}

A method's operating range is only useful if it is tested where the data
are, so we applied the procedure to every public dataset we could find that
met three requirements fixed in advance: a taxa-by-unit abundance matrix, a
measured ordered gradient per unit, and at least 40 units and 15 taxa.
Gradient variables were chosen from each dataset's metadata before download,
and all choices, inclusions and exclusions were registered before any
estimator ran. The second pass with the interaction test was registered
before it was applied to any of them, with the expectation---recorded in
advance and, as it turned out, wrong---that most would remain null because
their matrices are sparse. Table~\ref{tab:program} reports every dataset,
detected or not, through both instruments and both split grids.

grids disagree ($p = 0.005$ on the registered grid), and at $92\%$ zeros

\emph{Niwot} is the one design below the floor. Its 81 units sit under the
hundred-unit threshold at which Section~\ref{sec:mixloc} finds the test
anti-conservative, so although it rejects at $p = 0.005$ on both grids we
decline to count it as evidence. It is retained in the table because the
registration requires every dataset to be reported, not because we believe
it.

\paragraph{The split grid.} Amendment~5 fixed candidate splits at unit
quantiles $0.30$--$0.70$; the analyses use $0.15$--$0.85$, because on the
Everglades panel the narrow grid pinned the located split at its own
boundary. The deviation is recorded in the public pre-registration, and
because it is a deviation both grids are reported for every dataset. The
verdicts agree everywhere except Cedar Creek. The change cannot affect
validity---every permutation replicate minimizes over the same grid as the
observed statistic, so the null is the distribution of the same function of
the data---but it does affect the located split, which is why the procedure
does not use the interaction statistic to locate.

\paragraph{Time as the gradient, and why most of it is uninterpretable.}
The same second pass was applied with the gradient set to calendar time on
the thirteen registered long-term series (Table~\ref{tab:temporal}). Nine
reject. We report, and believe, three of them.

The reason is the design floor. Ten of the thirteen are annual series with
20 to 35 survey years---well below the hundred units at which the
interaction test becomes calibrated, and squarely in the range where
Section~\ref{sec:mixloc} measures false-positive rates of $0.17$ at
$P = 24$. Their $p$-values are not interpretable as evidence, and we do not
interpret them. Had we reported these as detections, the paper would have
claimed that structure reorganizes at a datable moment in seven of ten
long-term monitoring programmes, which we would now have to withdraw.

Three series clear the floor, because their programme samples five
occasions a year rather than one: the Everglades occasion-level series at
143--147 units. Two reject ($p = 0.005$ for Shark River Slough and for
Water Conservation Area) and one does not ($p = 0.060$ for Taylor Slough).
That is the honest temporal result, and it says what the first-generation
analysis said for a different reason: \emph{annual monitoring rarely samples
time densely enough for this instrument}. The remedy is not a better
statistic; it is more survey occasions.

\subsection{A published incumbent answer: the marsh--forest ecotone}
\label{sec:ecotone}

Every TITAN comparison above was regenerated by us on the same matrix, which
leaves open the objection that we tuned the incumbent's run. One dataset
removes that objection. \citet{payne2026ecotone} analyzed plant communities
across three coastal marsh--forest ecotones undergoing marsh migration in
New Jersey, New York and Massachusetts, and report TITAN community change
points at $0.8$ and $7.6$ PSU soil salinity, delimiting the ecotone. Those
numbers were published, by users of the incumbent method, before we saw the
data. We registered the analysis---gradient, unit, taxon columns, region
field, both split grids, and a compositional secondary---before downloading
the file, and the script that ran it prints whether the located split falls
inside $[0.8, 7.6]$, so there was no room afterwards to decide what counts
as agreement.

The data are 125 sampling points at three sites, percent cover in
$0.5\,\mathrm{m}^2$ quadrats, salinity $0.09$--$42.6$ PSU, with 20 taxa
passing the five-point occurrence rule.

\emph{Detection is unambiguous.} The interaction test rejects at
$p = 0.005$ on both split grids, and at $p = 0.005$ permuting within site,
which is the test of record here. Twenty taxa is near the bottom of the
range where the method works at all (Section~\ref{sec:limits}), and 125
units is just above the design floor, so this is a stronger result than the
design led us to expect.

\emph{Localization disagrees with the incumbent.} Per the two-stage rule the
location comes from the curve statistic, which places the structural change
at $14.2$ PSU ($p = 0.020$)---above both published change points, in
water that is already polyhaline. The interaction statistic's own split is
unstable, as Section~\ref{sec:mixloc} predicts it should be: $0.3$ PSU on
the widened grid and $0.37$ on the registered one, at the gradient's lower
edge. The registered compositional secondary, on centred-log-ratio cover,
detects equally strongly and places its split at $1.2$ PSU, inside TITAN's
interval, while its curve statistic no longer rejects ($p = 0.120$).

So the two channels do not agree about where this ecotone turns over, and
the transform changes which answer the interaction statistic gives. We
report the disagreement rather than choosing among the three numbers.
Section~\ref{sec:closure} is the reason we do not simply prefer the
log-ratio analysis: on null data it was the most anti-conservative cell we
measured. What this dataset establishes is the first half of the two-stage
claim---that structure reorganizes along this gradient, detected against an
independently published incumbent analysis---and it illustrates the second
half's fragility.

\section{Does structure change before composition?}
\label{sec:ordering}

The early-warning literature holds that changes in correlation structure can
precede the abundance collapses taxon-level methods detect
\citep{scheffer2009ews}. If that were general, a structural change point
would be a leading indicator---a boundary to act at before species are lost
rather than after---which is a claim with practical consequences, so we
registered a confirmatory test of it before its inputs existed
(amendment~6). Detection is decided by the interaction test; localization by
the curve statistic, chosen on the simulation of Section~\ref{sec:mixloc}
alone; the taxon-level change point is TITAN's $\mathrm{sum}(z-)$ on the
same unit-mean matrix; and the statistic is
\[
  R \;=\; \frac{z_{\mathrm{TITAN}} - \hat z}{\mathrm{IQR}(z)},
\]
positive when structure changes at the lower gradient value. The
confirmatory test is a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test of $R$ against
zero across independent detecting datasets, to run once, and only if at
least four qualified.

Seven qualified. The answer is no: $W = 12.0$, $p = 0.81$, with four of
seven positive and a median $R$ of $0.127$. \emph{Structure does not
systematically change before composition.} We report this as the registered
test's verdict and draw no ordering claim from the Everglades panel, whose
$R = 1.69$ is the largest in the set and which, taken alone, would have
supported one.

Two things are worth saying beside that null, neither of them registered and
both labelled accordingly.

First, the null is not a statement that the two channels agree. The
\emph{magnitudes} are large: median $|R| = 0.48$, mean $0.62$, and four of
seven exceed a quarter of the gradient's interquartile range. What fails is
the \emph{sign}, not the separation. Across these systems the structural and
compositional change points are typically half an interquartile range apart
and fall on either side with roughly equal frequency. That is a decoupling
result rather than a concordance one, and it is the more interesting reading
of the same numbers---but we did not register it, we cannot test it on the
data that produced it, and a median of seven unsigned quantities is thin
evidence for anything. We state it as a hypothesis for a future
pre-registration, not as a finding.

Second, one of the seven, Niwot, sits below the design floor of
Section~\ref{sec:mixloc}. The registered rule admitted it because the
detection rule was fixed in amendment~6 before the floor was measured, so
the test above includes it as registered. Removing it descriptively changes
nothing that matters: $W = 6.0$, $p = 0.44$, four of six positive, median
$R = 0.176$.

\paragraph{A premature run, disclosed.} This test was registered to run
once. It was in fact run twice. The first run happened while several
second-pass detection files were missing and TITAN's change point for one
dataset was still computing, so five rows were wrongly treated as
non-detecting; it gave a median $R$ of $0.039$ over four datasets with
$p = 1.00$. That output is preserved in the repository as
\texttt{31\_ordering\_incomplete.txt} and the deviation is recorded, dated,
in the public pre-registration. No specification changed between the two
runs---the estimator, detection rule, localization choice, statistic and
test are exactly those registered. We report both because discarding the
first would leave the record incomplete, and because the two agree: the
ordering hypothesis fails on the incomplete inputs and on the complete ones.

\section{Pre-registered application: the Butte Hill restoration network}
\label{sec:butte}

Montana Technological University's Native Plant Restoration Project has
monitored more than eighty long-term restoration sites across roughly six
hundred acres of the Butte Hill Superfund landscape since 2015: vegetation
surveys, soil chemistry, portable-XRF elemental screening, and multi-year
monitoring under real reclamation constraints. That is a taxa-by-plot
abundance matrix with plots orderable by measured soil metal concentration---the data shape of Section~\ref{sec:data}, with the gradient already in hand.

This section is a protocol, not a result, and we commit to it before seeing
outcomes. The estimator is exactly Section~\ref{sec:est}, with no free
parameters left: window rule, edge-count grid, pooled split statistic, $999$
permutations, $999$ bootstrap resamples. The analysis will report $\hat z$ on
the metal axis, its interval and width, and the permutation $p$; TITAN
\citep{baker2010titan} will be run on the same matrix and reported alongside,
per Section~\ref{sec:titan}. We pre-register one named secondary test in
addition to the primary free-split test: the pooled statistic $D(s)$
evaluated at the single split whose boundary lies nearest TITAN's decliner
change point on the same matrix, against the same plot-position permutation
null. Because TITAN's change point derives from the marginal channel---independent of ours by the results of Section~\ref{sec:titan}---anchoring
the split there spends no degrees of freedom of the covariance channel, and
the single-look test avoids the multiplicity cost of the free split. It is
the complementarity thesis run as a confirmatory test: \emph{does the
structure rewire where the marginals say the sensitive species exit?} A recovered threshold will be checked against
independent instruments the project already tracks---soil chemistry
breakpoints, diversity indices, and restoration outcomes---and against the
power table of Section~\ref{sec:power} before any claim is made; if the design
is in the regime where the test cannot reject, we will say so rather than
report a point estimate without evidence. The decade of repeat surveys is
load-bearing here: at 80 plots in a single survey year the test is weak, and
the multi-year analysis of Section~\ref{sec:power} shows that repeat
surveys restore power only when \emph{stacked}---plot-years as additional
window columns, permutation moving whole plots---and not when averaged.
The protocol therefore stacks years, and the number of surveyed years is
the first item of the data inventory.

Three requirements from Section~\ref{sec:limits} apply to that survey and
are committed to here. Cover or biomass must be recorded, not presence and
absence: on sparse incidence the method has under a quarter of its nominal
power, and a null would be uninterpretable. The metal gradient at Butte
Hill is spatially structured, because deposition and drainage follow the
landscape, so the within-region permutation is the test of record and
plot-level permutation is reported only beside it. And with roughly eighty
plots in any one year the design sits near the lower edge of the range
where the test has power, so a null will be reported as a bounded null,
with the power table as its bound, rather than as evidence of no
threshold.

Four survey-specific choices cannot be fixed until the data inventory is in
hand, and each will be fixed from documentation alone and recorded in a
dated amendment before any curve is computed: the numbers of plots, taxa, and
survey years; the gradient variable $z$ (a single metal, or the first
principal component of the measured metals); the taxonomic reconciliation
across years; and the treatment of plots missing from some years.

\section{Discussion}

The method locates where community co-occurrence structure reorganizes along a
gradient; it does not identify which taxa drive the reorganization, which is
precisely what TITAN does well. Section~\ref{sec:titan} shows the
complementarity is real and runs in both directions, so the recommendation is
to report the two together---and to treat their disagreement as a
measurement of what kind of change the gradient drives, not as a conflict to
be resolved. Section~\ref{sec:curvature} qualifies the first clause: the
curvature decomposition does name taxa, exactly and without extra
machinery, and on the Everglades panel it names a set disjoint from TITAN's
indicators. What it does not supply is a taxon-level \emph{change point},
which is what TITAN's construction is for.

The one general ecological claim we registered did not survive. If
structural change systematically preceded compositional change, a
structural threshold would be an early-warning boundary and the practical
case for reading the covariance channel would make itself. Across seven
independent detecting systems it does not: the ordering is as often one way
as the other ($p = 0.81$; Section~\ref{sec:ordering}). We think the result
is worth more than its null verdict suggests, for a reason we are careful
not to overstate. The two change points are not close together and then
unordered; they are typically half an interquartile range apart, with the
sign varying. If that survives a test designed for it---which the present
one was not, and which cannot honestly be run on the data that suggested
it---then the covariance channel is not a leading indicator of the
compositional one but an independent axis of community response, and the
practical recommendation to run both instruments follows more strongly than
from complementarity alone. We flag it as the obvious next pre-registration
rather than as a result.

Four limitations bound the claim. First, the Euler characteristic is
label-invariant: a reorganization that permutes which taxa occupy which
modules, while preserving the number, size, and arrangement of the modules,
changes the shape of nothing and is invisible here; if each taxon's abundance
profile is also unchanged, taxon-level methods miss it too, and detecting it
requires comparing the correlation matrices entry by entry. Second, the permutation test detects
association of structure with the gradient, not sharpness; and our attempt to
calibrate the bootstrap interval width as a sharpness diagnostic failed at
realistic design sizes (Section~\ref{sec:power}), so the method currently
distinguishes \emph{where} structure changes fastest, not whether the change
is discontinuous. The interaction test does not repair this: it too measures
association, and a smooth structural trend separates the two clouds much as
a sharp one does. A test of sharpness compatible with the connectance
control remains open, and we now think the obstacle is not the choice of
statistic but the resolution limit of the next paragraph. Third, localization resolution is set by the window
width, hence by survey size; the power analysis of Section~\ref{sec:power} is
the price list, and the public-data negative in
Section~\ref{sec:public} is what paying insufficient attention to it looks
like. Fourth, the permutation test treats plots as exchangeable along the
gradient. Where community structure is spatially autocorrelated and the
gradient is spatially structured---as a smelter plume makes it on Butte
Hill---the test is anti-conservative, and restricted permutations within
spatial blocks are the appropriate remedy. Both instruments need them: the
simulations of Sections~\ref{sec:limits} and~\ref{sec:mixloc} are precisely
where plot-level permutation over-rejects, and every field analysis here
reports the within-region test as the test of record wherever a region field
exists.

Two practical conclusions follow from the operating range. The first is
about data type: the method reads correlations, and sparse counting
destroys them, so cover, biomass or dense counts are the substrate to use,
and an incidence matrix is close to hopeless. The second is about
resampling: where a gradient is spatially structured, permuting plots
freely inflates false positives two- to threefold, and a within-region
permutation should be reported as a matter of course.

A better pooling statistic exists. The registered statistic averages
$|$Cohen $d|$ across the connectance grid, but those grid points are nearly
collinear, and a whitened cumulative-sum statistic over the window sequence
detects more at the same false-positive rate ($0.68$ against $0.57$ at
$P = 160$; $0.33$ against $0.25$ at $P = 80$) and localizes slightly
better. We report this as a post-hoc improvement and leave every result in
this paper on the registered statistic; the gain should be confirmed on
data registered in advance before it is adopted.

The Euler characteristic itself is a choice, not a commitment. The
contributions this paper actually rests on---the connectance confound and
its control, the window--split--permutation machinery, and the
complementarity results---are summary-agnostic, and Section~\ref{sec:swap}
runs the identical estimator over Betti curves, modularity, and distribution
summaries. What $\chi$ specifically buys is an integer computable by clique
counting with no persistence computation, a stability guarantee
\citep{dlotko2023eulercurves}, no dimension limit on the flag complex, and
continuity with a validated line of transition-localization work
\citep{sharma2026ecs,koenig2026churn}; a practitioner who prefers a different
stable curve-valued summary loses nothing else in the pipeline. We consider
this modularity a feature of the method rather than a hedge: the claim under
test on a new gradient is never ``the Euler characteristic matters'' but
``community structure reorganizes here,'' and any summary that can carry that
signal at matched connectance is admissible evidence.

The construction extends naturally where several measurement types exist on
the same plots---vegetation, soil chemistry, molecular data---by fusing
per-modality distance matrices with multiple kernel learning, which is mature
technology \citep{mariette2018mixkernel} and, in the form used in the
motivating pipe-flow work, inherits the stability of its topological
components \citep{koenig2026churn}. Euler characteristics have also served as
feature extractors inside supervised multi-omics pipelines
\citep{jain2024deepida}. We deliberately keep both out of this paper: the
vegetation-only threshold is the clean claim, and fusion belongs to the
follow-up where it can be tested rather than asserted.

\section{Conclusions}

Community co-occurrence structure changes along environmental gradients in
most of the systems we could test, and where it changes is not where
taxon-level methods say composition changes. Establishing that required two
things this paper supplies and one it does not.

The first is a summary that measures arrangement rather than density.
Indexing an Euler characteristic filtration by edge count rather than by
similarity is the whole of it, and without that control the topological
summary is a connectance measurement wearing a different coordinate.

The second is enough statistical power to see the signal at survey scale.
The windowed split statistic, pre-registered and honest, detects in two of
nine public gradients; recoding the same data so that taxa rather than
windows are the sample detects in seven, and detects on the incumbent's own
benchmark where the windowed statistic cannot. The gain is instrumental, not
a matter of dataset choice: same matrices, same preprocessing, same
permutation principle.

What this paper does not supply is a test of \emph{thresholdness}. Both
instruments measure association between structure and gradient; neither
distinguishes an abrupt reorganization from a steep smooth one at the design
sizes surveys afford, and our attempt to calibrate the bootstrap interval
width for that purpose failed. A survey that needs to classify its gradient
response as threshold-like rather than gradual needs a different instrument,
or many more plots, than anything demonstrated here.

We would rather the operating range be read as the paper's main practical
contribution than the detections. An instrument whose failures are
uncharacterized is not evidence; one whose failures are mapped can be
trusted within them. Ours fails on sparse incidence data, fails below
roughly a hundred units, over-rejects by a factor of two to three when a spatially
structured gradient is permuted freely, and localizes no better than its window width
allows. Inside those bounds, it sees something the standard toolkit does
not.

\section{Open Research}
\label{sec:avail}

All analysis code, simulation scripts, result logs and the dated
pre-registration with its amendments and deviation records are archived at
\url{https://github.com/sushovan4/ecs-community-thresholds} and deposited
with a permanent identifier on Zenodo (\textsc{doi to be assigned on
acceptance}). Public input datasets are obtained from their original
repositories by the included fetch script and are not redistributed; each is
cited in Table~\ref{tab:program} or in the text. The Euler characteristic
machinery builds on GUDHI \citep{maria2014gudhi}, and the intersection
profile on the reference implementation accompanying
\citet{kawamura2026intersection}. The reference implementation is Python; an
R interface, \texttt{ecsurf}, wraps it through \texttt{reticulate} and
reproduces the Everglades analysis in four lines, with the stacked
repeat-survey design exposed through a \texttt{groups} argument. TITAN
comparisons use the R package TITAN2.

\paragraph{Reproducibility note.} Alpha complexes are computed with GUDHI's
\texttt{safe} predicates. An earlier version of this work used
\texttt{fast}, which employs inexact geometric predicates; on taxon clouds
where many taxa share identical sparse occurrence patterns---and therefore
identical correlation profiles---the underlying Delaunay construction
resolves the resulting ties differently across linear-algebra backends. One
dataset returned different statistics on two machines with identical seeds,
and \texttt{fast} disagreed with \texttt{exact} by more than $50\%$ at one
split. \texttt{safe} agrees with \texttt{exact} to ten decimal places at
roughly $0.7$ of its cost. Every result reported here was recomputed under
\texttt{safe}; the superseded outputs are retained in the repository, and
the correction is recorded in the public pre-registration. Practitioners
applying any Alpha-complex method to sparse community data should not use
inexact predicates.
